# P2-P26 — What survives Ross, Lepper and Hubbard's discrediting intervention?

[Ross, Lepper & Hubbard (1975), pp. 880–892](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1HMAn90P7rF_dKZV5yvzsK2JLb9WYXq6x/view) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.32.5.880).

Experiment 1 assigned 60 female high-school juniors to feedback portraying high, ordinary or low performance on judging which note in a pair was authentic. The feedback was manipulated and actual task scores averaged about 14 of 25 across conditions. After the disclosure, estimated own scores were 17.05, 15.85 and 12.75 (Table 1). Estimates of future performance and ratings of specific skills still differed across feedback groups. The 5- versus 25-minute wait did not strengthen the effect as predicted. The measured persistence is an effect on reported judgments within the experiment.

Experiment 2 analyzes 72 Stanford women as actors and 72 paired observers, excluding 24 additional actors studied without an observer. It distinguishes no debrief, outcome debrief, and outcome plus process debrief. An observer heard the explanation given to the actor through the observation setup. Under outcome debrief, both roles still differed by earlier success/failure feedback across several measures. After process debrief, observers' perceived initial score (16.92 versus 12.00) and task ability (5.25 versus 3.83) still differed at the conventional level, while the analogous actor comparisons were no longer conventionally significant. The authors say the relevant actor–observer interactions did not reach significance (pp. 887–888). The pattern motivates a question about who receives a correction and under what conditions. It does not identify a statistically established difference in treatment effect between roles.

Ross et al. explicitly theorize that derivatives can appear independent after a source is discredited, because early feedback shaped the interpretation of concurrent cues (pp. 889–891). General psychological persistence and apparently independent reasons are therefore strong antecedents. A NOMOS study needs to track whether a specific institutional recipient changed its stated reasons, actually relied on earlier derivative versions, or had authority to reuse a valid fact for the later purpose. A disposition, a change in the person's own belief and a sender's correction receipt measure different events. Demand characteristics remain an alternative explanation for apparent success of process debriefing.
