## Actual claims supported by the newly held originals

**Rider, 2006:** On pp. 213–214, Rider contrasts an overly systematic reading of canonical philosophy (positions separated from the individual thinker) with an overly historicizing reading (the thinker reduced to social milieu). She argues that historic work matters to a present thinker when it reopens the *doubts and real problems* motivating past philosophical thought, rather than only cataloguing doctrines. At pp. 214–216, Foucault's initially unaccepted thesis proposal and Nietzsche's criticized *Birth of Tragedy* illustrate the difficulty of fitting a question that scrutinizes a discipline's foundations into the discipline's established categories. Rider also recognizes the critic's legitimate disciplinary concerns: Wilamowitz may have been right **as a judge of philological scholarship**, even if the larger philosophical project mattered in another way. Her closing section distinguishes internal philosophical self-discipline from institutional duties such as assessment, course design and appointment. These examples do NOT show that academic rejection proves philosophical originality, or that evaluative standards should be abolished.

**Tripodi, 2016:** Sections 1–3, pp. 216–224, engage Diego Marconi's 2014 book, specialization, scholarly professionalism and discipline-internal evaluative criteria; the views of Marconi are reported **through Tripodi** until the original book is acquired. In section 4, pp. 225–229, she treats academic credibility as socially situated. Her example of women philosophers concerns a possible *unfair loss of testimonial credibility* and limited ability to participate as recognized knowledge producers. She explicitly invokes Miranda Fricker's testimonial and hermeneutical epistemic injustice (pp. 226–227). Thus neither the social credibility problem nor those terms originate with Tripodi or NOMOS. The argument does not establish that any particular peer review or promotion decision was biased without case-specific evidence.

## Return to the original philosophical problem

The founding NOMOS question concerns the illicit expansion of a situated episode into a stable judgment about a person and the institution's claim of authority to make that expansion. The new pair is a *bounded analogy*, not equivalent institutional jurisdiction: a philosophy department must judge a thesis's methodological merits, but this is not automatically a warrant to decide the author's entire capacity to be a philosopher. Conversely, the mere rejection of an unconventional thesis is not proof of unjust exclusion.

Do not collapse these three things:

1. **Claim-relative evidential credibility:** whether the actual argument, testimony and defeaters warrant this specific proposition.
2. **Domain expertise / methodological fit:** whether a reviewer has grounds to regard an author as competent on a specific problem or to reject a thesis under disclosed standards.
3. **Person-wide and institutional status:** whether the first two warrant durable classification of the bearer, and whether anyone may impose consequences beyond the immediate disciplinary judgment.

These distinctions are continuous with NOMOS 0.752's claim-specific bearer relevance and the 0.866 stigma/labeling lineage; they are not new legal rules. The key question is when a local criticism is legitimately bounded and when it becomes an unsupported person-target judgment.

## Adversarial research plan and limits

First compare Rider's Nietzsche/Wilamowitz example to a **genuine negative control**: a methodologically defective but ambitious philosophical text. If NOMOS automatically dignifies any rejection as gatekeeping injustice, it fails. Second compare Tripodi's social-identity account to a **legitimate expertise control**: a credential may be imperfect but relevant evidence of claim-specific skill; such evidence is not categorically forbidden. Third, consider an equal-content evaluation design varying affiliation/credential labels, collecting separate ratings for argument merit, speaker credibility, person-wide category and exclusion consequence. That is only a *prospective design*, and social-psychology literature and Fricker must be treated as strong prior alternatives. No human-subject experiment or data reanalysis is claimed.

**Research-director conclusion:** preserve the actual question, not a growing archive of derivative checklists. The strongest next paper is still a close reading of *L'Étranger* (how an action supports, fails to support, or is made to support a person-wide inference), with carefully selected real institutions as limiting comparisons. Rider and Tripodi add a **reflexive test on our own research methodology**: procedural verification is not the subject of philosophical inquiry and a large research corpus does not itself constitute a new theory.
