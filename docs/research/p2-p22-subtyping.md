# P2-P22 — Accepted counterexamples and subtyping

Kunda & Oleson (1995), *JPSP* 68(4):565–579, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.68.4.565), [filed PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1cXQoE6_P6y3ynJjR7Rk7isSrcH5LzWAE/view). Studies 1–3 compare whether a neutral additional attribute changes how a counterstereotypic individual is taken to speak for the group. In Studies 2–3 the added trait itself came to be seen as atypical and associated with the deviance. Study 4 addresses alternative similarity, variance and pragmatic accounts by comparing stereotype-consistent and inconsistent targets; the same added attribute did not have a uniform effect. Read pp. 573–577 with the General Discussion. The study concerns generalization from an individual to a group, and the authors explicitly distinguish it from stereotype effects on an individual (pp. 577–578).

The generic claim that an accepted counterexample can be redescribed so the original evaluation survives has prior art. In an institution's written reasons, test whether a replacement source is independent and whether the replacement proposition still answers the same thing. Kunda and Oleson's scope contrast is prior art for asking that question, not evidence about any particular agency.
