# P2-P28 — An inconsistent act can be accepted and still lose evidentiary force

[Hayden & Mischel (1976), pp. 109–132](https://drive.google.com/file/d/13NzgqbpT-X3tWgkQrFq1elGlQ3CNk2U5/view) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.1976.tb00587.x). Close reading covers both experiments, Tables 1–7 and the discussion.

Experiment I recruited 44 high-school seniors and reports 42 after exclusions (p. 113). Participants first received descriptions that supported an aggressive or submissive impression. Groups then attributed motivation to the same 15 cartoons, five in each of three behavior classes, assigned to an aggressive character, a submissive character or someone without a supplied impression. For submissive cartoons, the aggressive-impression group attributed more aggressive motivation than the no-impression group (Table 3: 7.43 against 3.29; pp. 119–120). The match between the cartoon's behavior and the rated motivation was the dominant pattern. Motive and initial-impression contrasts are mixed; the author flags post hoc subgroup comparisons for submissive motivation despite the nonsignificant interaction. The data do not show a uniform override of new evidence by first impressions.

Experiment II used a separate sample of 42. Each cartoon received one of four attributions: situation, temporary concealment of the real self, real self or no particular reason. The impression-by-behavior interaction was reported at F(4,78)=13.13, p<.01. In Table 4, the same aggressive cartoons are attributed to the real self on average 3.50 times under an aggressive impression, 0.36 under a submissive impression and 1.86 with no supplied impression, out of five. In the two impression groups pooled in Table 6, the concealment option averages 2.75 for inconsistent behavior, 0.57 for consistent behavior and 0.93 for neutral behavior. The situational-pressure choice did not significantly distinguish these three types (pp. 125–129). The experiment measures interpretation of shown actions, with different participant samples and response measures across experiments. It does not establish that every participant applied both mechanisms or that a true prior source was withdrawn.

For NOMOS, the observable hypotheses are now separate. An authority may dispute that an event happened. It may accept the event and interpret the motive to fit its existing assessment. It may also accept and describe the event accurately while limiting its relevance to a given inference. A further explanation can treat the event as strategic concealment, a claim that requires its own support. A document audit can track these public moves without diagnosing the reviewer. Kunda and Oleson already distinguish subtyping and stereotype preservation, and [Ross et al.](p2-p26-ross-debriefing.md) directly discredit an initial source. Record-level institutional use and authority remain the candidate extension.
